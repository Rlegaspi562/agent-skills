## Key takeaways

1. **Story as chapters:** a brand is a series of chapters inside one macro story. Good content hits emotion, value or curiosity.
2. **Macro picture first:** who you are, where you started, what you believe, who it is for, why you, where you are going, what the payoff is. That makes the ICP obvious.
3. **Reverse-engineer from the end:** state the end goal, then the beliefs or chapters that lead there (Wispr Flow "Jarvis", Tesla Secret Master Plan). Product roadmap = story roadmap; for a personal brand, your own development is the story.
4. **Voice:** decide personality, what you would never say, tone (Duolingo owl). Be authentic, avoid AI-sounding copy.
5. **Vehicles:** rented (reach), owned (trust, conversion), earned (mindshare). Start with 1 to 2 platforms where the audience already is and that fit writing vs talking vs camera. YouTube compounds hardest; LinkedIn underrated for B2B; X for testing ideas fast.
6. **Packaging 50/50 with content:** hooks (bold claim, callout, curiosity gap, clear outcome, contrarian), clear titles, minimal thumbnails, first 3 seconds with clean setup and on-screen text.
7. **Formatting:** platform native and scannable.
8. **Growth tips:** engage to build network, steal formats not content, make 5 more versions of outliers, point every channel to another, turn one idea into many.

### Building a Story to Tell

If you take away only one thing from this article, make it this: building and distributing a brand is about telling a series of "chapters" that fit into a broader, macro story.

In other words, you’re taking your audience on a journey, and every piece of organic content is another step along the way (this is why founder-led content is crushing right now). 

This storytelling framework is also the key to building a brand with longevity and avoiding burnout, a bored/fatigued audience, and other issues that creators/brands often face.

Ultimately, good storytelling:

- Makes people feel something (emotion) 
- Teaches people something (value)
- Makes people want more (curiosity)
Of course, there are other important ingredients, but generally speaking, if you approach content through these three buckets, you will attract an audience. 

How to craft your story 

1. Start with the macro picture 
Start with a high-level overview of what you want people to buy into. 

What do you want them to believe? How do you want your audience to feel? What specific pain points are you solving? Why should they listen to you?

Get clear on: 

```
Who are you?               → What you do 
Where did you start?       → Your origin, the "before"
What do you believe?       → Broader thesis
Who are you doing it for?  → Your audience and their struggle
Why you?                   → Your edge, experience and authority 
Where are you going?       → The mission, the big vision, final dest.
What's the payoff?         → Your product, brand or community
```

Once you get clear on these questions, it becomes much easier to build an ICP (ideal customer profile) and target them through organic (or paid) channels. 

2.     Reverse-engineering the story

Once you're clear on your brand's end goals, start at the END of your story, then reverse-engineer the chapters and beliefs you want to instill in people.

For example, @WisprFlow’s end goal is a real-life “Jarvis.” The ultimate computer voice assistant that buys back the time you waste typing. They’re not there yet, but that’s the point. Every piece of content takes you one step closer, and the product gets better along the way.

Every chapter they tell points back to that end goal:

```
The end     → Jarvis (voice runs your computer)
Belief 1    → Typing is slow and outdated
Belief 2    → Voice is the most natural way to work
Belief 3    → You'll be more [productive] by using [Wispr]

```

Another great example is @Tesla.

Back in 2006, Elon published Tesla’s famous “Secret Master Plan.” He told everyone the end goal upfront, then laid out the chapters to get there:

```
The end     → Sustainable energy for everyone
Chapter 1   → Build an expensive sports car (Roadster)
Chapter 2   → Use that money to build a cheaper car (Model S)
Chapter 3   → Use that money to build an affordable car (Model 3)
Chapter 4   → Add solar and clean power generation
```

Many people followed the brand/product just to see if Elon could get there. He seemed so crazy that it sparked massive curiosity, and people felt compelled to follow the story. 

As I’ve said in previous articles, brand and product are closely tied. Your product roadmap IS your story roadmap. Every new feature, launch, or milestone is another chapter toward reaching the end state.

For those building a personal brand, your personal development IS the story. The value, insights, and experiences you share as you try to reach [end goal].

Or maybe you've already reached a goal, and you're showing others the roadmap to get there. In that case, you're sharing the story you've already lived, instead of developing it in real time.

Note that it's often a mix of both, and branding is super dynamic. Over time, your story will change, but it's still a good idea to establish a high-level overview of the end goal. 

3.     Finding your voice 

Your story is what you say, but your voice is how you say it.

- What's my personality? 
- What would I never say?
- What tonality, grammar, and brand voice will I use?
For example, @duolingo could’ve been just another language app. Instead, they leaned into an unhinged, chaotic owl and became one of the most viral brands online. 

For personal brands, my advice is to be as authentic to yourself as possible. Talk in your normal voice and don't try to cater too much to styles that don't fit who you actually are. Also, avoid using AI. 

Do you want to be perceived as intellectual? Funny? etc. Your brand voice should match the perception you want to project. 

TL;DR of crafting your story: 

1. Get clear on the macro picture and high-level end goal of your brand
1. With the end goal in mind, reverse-engineer the specific chapters 
1. Establish your voice and how you want to present those chapters

### Selecting Vehicles 

A vehicle is just a channel you use to distribute your story and content. X, YouTube, a newsletter, a podcast, etc.

Every vehicle falls into one of three buckets:

1. Rented: Platforms you don't control (X, TikTok, IG, YouTube) - best for reach
1. Owned: Audiences you have direct access to (newsletter, community, website) - for trust and conversion
1. Earned: Other people's audiences (collabs, podcasts) - for mindshare and network effect 
To build mass distribution, you need all three. It's a combination of top-, middle-, and bottom-of-funnel content that tells the story you established above. 

How I think about the various platforms: 

```
Vehicle       Best for              Funnel     Effort
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
X             Ideas, network        TOF/MOF    Low-Med
Short-form    Raw reach             TOF        Medium
YouTube       Depth, trust, search  TOF/MOF/BOF    High
LinkedIn      B2B, professionals    TOF/MOF    Low-Med
Newsletter    Trust, conversion     MOF/BOF    Medium
Podcasts      Authority, depth      MOF        High (own) / Low (guest)
Community     Retention, loyalty    BOF        High
```

How to pick 

Don't try to be everywhere at once. Just start with 1-2 platforms max.

Ask yourself:

- Where does my audience already spend time?
e.g., if you're a tech founder, LinkedIn or X is probably a good place to start. If you're a personal brand, YouTube is probably best. 

- Am I better at writing, talking, or being on camera?
- How much time can I realistically commit to producing content? 
Each platform - summarized: 

1. 𝕏
X is the best platform for building a network and testing ideas fast. You can test ideas here and extrapolate them to other channels.

```
Funnel   → TOF / MOF
Effort   → Low to medium
Suits    → Writers, founders, operators
```

2.     Short-form video (TikTok, Reels, Shorts)

Good for personal brands and those who want to grow fast.

```
Funnel   → TOF
Effort   → Medium
Suits    → Anyone comfortable on camera (or good at editing)
```

3.     YouTube (long-form)

Slow to start, but it compounds like nothing else. By far the strongest distribution channel. 

```
Funnel   → TOF/ MOF / BOF
Effort   → High
Suits    → Educators, storytellers
```

4.     LinkedIn

Underrated. Less competition than X, and the audience has buying power. If you’re B2B, this should be on your list.

```
Funnel   → TOF / MOF
Effort   → Low to medium
Suits    → Anyone selling to businesses

```

5.     Newsletter

Really good outlet for being authentic. Also one of the few vehicles that gets the highest engagement immediately after posting. Traditional organic content, by contrast, has a growth curve over time. 

```
Funnel   → MOF / BOF
Effort   → Medium
Suits    → Everyone (eventually)
```

6.     Podcasts

Podcasts allow you to build deep trust, give more nuance to your content, and allow you to "borrow" an audience. 

```
Funnel   → MOF
Effort   → High (your own) / Low (guesting)
Suits    → Talkers, interviewers
```

7.     Community (Discord, Telegram, Skool, etc.)

Interact with your community on a more personal level.

```
Funnel   → BOF
Effort   → High
Suits    → Brands with an engaged core audience
```

You don't need all of these channels at the start. Again, focus on where your existing audience is (biggest TAM), and what formats you're most comfortable with. I like writing, so I personally focus on 𝕏.

Every vehicle has a different job in telling your story. Rented platforms get you discovered; owned platforms build trust; and owned communities let you be more personal and 1-on-1 with the audience. 

Depending on the brand you're building, you'll ideally have a mix of these platforms, and you can present different pieces of your story on each.

For example, X might be more product-focused, while Instagram is more lifestyle, and your community is extremely personal.

### Packaging & Formatting

This is where most brands struggle. 

You can have the best story in the world and the perfect vehicle to tell it, but still get 200 views.

In my experience, packaging is the most underrated skill in content. Most people spend 95% of their time on the content itself and 5% on the packaging. It should be closer to 50/50.

Packaging is:

1.     Hooks

Hooks are what draw people into your content, and they're the most important piece to master.

A good hook usually does at least one of these:

- Bold claim 
- Callout
- Curiosity gap
- Clear outcome 
- Contrarian 
2.     Titles

Big promise, clear outcome, and no confusion.

If someone even has to think about what your title means, you've lost them. Clarity is everything. 

3.     Thumbnails & visuals

The only job of a visual/thumbnail is to get people to click. The less, the better. 

4.     The first 3 seconds (video)

For video, the hook isn’t just the words. It’s the visuals and the speaker's presentation. 

- Have a clean background/setup 
- Editing/visuals (if applicable) 
- Use text on screen to reinforce the hook 
Formatting 

1. Be "native"
Every platform has its quirks and its own language. Spend time consuming viral formats and thinking about how you can apply them to your content.

For X, that might mean articles. For LinkedIn, it might be longer-style posts that tap more into your personal story. 

2.     Make it scannable

Generally speaking, scannable content is very good. Infographics, bullet points, concise copy, etc. 

Make your content easy enough that a 10 y/o can understand.

### Final Advice & Recap

So, to recap the process of building distribution from scratch: 

1. Craft your story: Macro picture, end goal, chapters & voice
1. Pick your vehicle: 1-2 platforms that suit you best with the biggest TAM
1. Packaging: Learn the hooks, titles, and visuals for that vehicle
1. Formatting: Platform native, scannable, easy to consume 
A few growth tips I've picked up:

- Build your network. Don't just think about posting - actually engage with others on the platform (especially on written platforms like X)  
- Steal formats (not content). Pay attention to adjacent niches and try pulling concepts into yours. 
- Double down on outliers. When something pops, make 5 more versions. Don't think, "I already did it."
- Point every channel to another. X to newsletter, YouTube to X, and so on.
- Turn one idea into many. Repurpose as much as you can. One article can become material for other distribution channels.
